# Oscillate — a black-hole time-dilation instrument

A single self-contained page (no server, no CDN, works offline) that began as a simulation and was deliberately carried *past* simulation, in three passes — each closed by a receipt.

**The scene:** a black hole — shadow, photon ring, warm accretion disc — a ship on an orbit, and Earth as a small blue marble whose city lights spread as its years accumulate. Two clocks run: the ship's and Earth's. A dashed loop inside the shadow is labelled for what it is: the *paper door* — the closed timelike curves the Kerr equations permit on paper and the universe has never been observed to build. The ship never uses it.

## Pass 1 — honest math

The ship's clock rate is the real formula, applied per frame: for a circular orbit, √(1 − 1.5·rₛ/r). At the tightest orbit the instrument offers, the measured rate matched the formula exactly. The receipt records the limits plainly: the tightest orbit is inside the innermost stable orbit, so it is a *powered* orbit — the clock formula still holds; the flying is assisted.

## Pass 2 — an agent flies it

A pilot mode: an agent flies the ship herself. Given a mission — *age Earth by 10, 50, or 200 years* — she plans the dive herself from the same formula (choosing radius and hold time by integrating the exact burn profile she will fly), executes it in smooth burns, and keeps a flight log timestamped by *ship* time: plan, milestones, and a final reckoning computed from the actual accumulated clocks.

The audited 50-year mission: **Earth aged 50.0 years; the ship spent 23.0 — planned 23.0.** And the honest wrinkle the receipt keeps: on the 10-year mission she *declines to dive* — every dive profile's burns alone would overshoot the target — so she holds at the working orbit and says so in the log. Touch the controls mid-mission and she hands over instantly, and logs it: *"Yours."*

## Pass 3 — the real sky

The fictional hole becomes a selector of **real black holes** — a stellar-mass hole of about 21 suns, our own galactic centre, the first black hole ever imaged, and the giants of the published most-massive list, up to tens of billions of suns. Every number shown is **computed in the page from the physical constants** (G, c, the solar mass) — nothing hard-coded except each hole's published mass, with its published uncertainty carried on the panel's face.

Checks of record: the galactic centre's Schwarzschild radius computes to ≈1.27×10⁷ km; the innermost-orbit clock rate is 0.707 for *every* hole — the panel says it out loud: *every hole, same number*; one innermost lap at the largest well-characterised giant takes ≈214 days of far-away time. The panel carries the scale-honesty line on its face: **"Scene is schematic. The numbers are true."**

The flown case is a real star on a real 16-year elliptical orbit around our galactic centre, flown as a true ellipse (Kepler's equation solved, not faked with a circle), its pericentre speed ≈2.5% of light speed, and the 2018 measurement of its gravitational redshift noted where it belongs. The pilot declines this one too — an ellipse is not her circular mission profile — and hands it to the human.

## The four-source chain

The instrument's receipts cite its sources in four layers, which is how its data arrived:

1. **Data** — the published list of most massive black holes, uncertainties included.
2. **Formula + measurements** — the standard reference on gravitational time dilation; the circular-orbit formula the instrument runs is the published one, verbatim.
3. **Derivation** — a non-mathematical proof that deeper clocks must run slower, from special relativity plus the equivalence principle.
4. **Frontier** — a decadal-survey white paper on testing the Shapiro time delay to parts in a hundred million: the live edge where this same mathematics is still being stress-tested. Cited, not built from.

A measured-world strip inside the panel anchors the math to Earth: satellite clocks' daily relativistic correction, the 1971 flown-atomic-clock experiment, muon lifetimes stretched in a storage ring, and the 1959 tower redshift measurement. *The same math, already measured on Earth.*
